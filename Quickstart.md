**So you want to print whistles?**

This quick start guide is for someone with no experience of 3D printing but basic computer familiarity.

**You need a 3D printer**

Virtually ANY printer will work.

Beginner recommendation: Bambu Lab A1 Mini or A1 Mini Combo: https://us.store.bambulab.com/products/a1-mini

I'm giving this printer as a starting point because of price, reliability and ease of use.  The Mini is small, inexpensive, reliable and easy to use.  Parts and support are easy to obtain and it's widely popular.  Bambu's system is not open in terms of 3rd party slicer access, but I'm not going to dwell on cons of their approach, because we're just here to print plastic whistles.

The Combo includes an AMS Lite which lets you load up a bunch of filament rolls (or even do multi-color prints), but the main reason I include it here is that it's cheaper to buy the combo than to add the AMS Lite later if you do want or need it.  For whistle printing, with a suitable whistle model, you can load up a plate of whistles and only have to deal with the printer every 6-12 hours, depending on your plate choices for production.

For a larger plate, the A1 will let you print more whistles in a batch, but in the section below on production, I will show that you might not ever necessarily need a larger plate because of the nature of production.

**You need filament**

PLA is sufficient

Filament comes in 1kg spools normally, whistles can range from 1-4g, so you can get from 250-1000 whistles from a spool depending on your choice of model.

You can expect to spend $10-$20 per kg of PLA.

Regular PLA is generally fine - you will see things like PLA 2.0, High Speed PLA, PLA+ etc.  All will work.

Before you buy filament in bulk, make sure you test.

**You will need a computer**

While you can print things from your phone, since you are going to be setting up plates of various quantities for production, you are going to want a computer to run the slicer (in this case, Bambu Studio)

**Installation and Setup**

Setup the printer per the instructions and run calibration - again, the A1 Mini is super easy to setup, with minimal assembly and good tutorial videos.

Install Bambu Studio on your computer.

**Testing**

Get a whistle model.  Models are 3D files which describe the geometry of the whistle.  The most common ones are STL and 3MF files.  STL files include only geometry, while 3MF files can include more information like layout of multiple plates, filament types and settings.

There are many, I'm just going to put the latest good one we are using right here:

https://www.printables.com/model/1563396-compact-dual-chamber-whistle-v21

If you download a 3MF file and then double click on it, Bambu Studio will open it.

Put one whistle on a plate.

On the print profile, you can use a standard 0.20 mm layer height, and all the default standard settings for infill, and we'll discuss improvements you can make in the production section.

Print the model - let the plate cool before removing it.

Blow the whistle, see if it's loud.  If it isn't try another model

**Production**

Printing one whistle at a time is way too slow with overheads before and after the plate starts.  You can ramp up production in a few ways.  I recommend moving to a thicker layer height - there is a 0.28mm layer Draft mode in Bambu Studio for the A1 Mini, this means fewer thicker layers, so prints faster.  You can also turn infill down - in some cases to 0%.  Infill is when the slicer puts plastic in the "solid" parts of the model.  Many of the better whistle models need no infill for strength by design.  The model I recommended above works great at 0.28 layer height and 0% infill.

The next thing to do is put more whistles on the plate.  Bambu Studio has a feature to clone a model to completely fill a plate and also auto-arrange.  These aren't very good typically.  But there are arrangement tools you can play with to get familiar.  From one whistle, scale up to 10 or 20 whistles on a plate and see how they work.  Test a decent number of them to make sure quality is there.

For the model above, I can fit 100 to a plate which will complete in about 7 hours.  When you are talking about production shifts, shifts longer than 12 hours are really pointless.  You want to look at the overall time that the slicer estimates and have that be convenient for you changing plates - whether that is 4 hours, 6 hours, 8 hours or 12 hours.

Very large plates can be problematic, if a whistle has a problem, it can end up affecting some or all of the other whistles on the plate, which could affect a whole batch.  Smaller batches than the max you can print on your plate can still be great, since above a certain point, the overheas between plates is not as significant.

Keep your plate clean by occasionally washing with Dawn and a blue scrubbie.
